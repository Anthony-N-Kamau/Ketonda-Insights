    Capital at risk

    A range, not a false precision: the weighted exposure, and the high side if every likely risk lands.

    Methodology

    The arithmetic is on the page, not in a black box.

    Two things are estimates and one is arithmetic. The probabilities and impacts are judgements produced by a model from your description; the way they combine is fixed, published and applied identically to every plan. Separating those two is the point of this section.

    Calculation rules v2026.1
    Exposure
    probability × impact ÷ 100
    A probability-weighted loss, in points of starting capital.
    Overall score
    Σ exposure, capped at 100
    The sum of every risk's exposure. A score of 45 means 45 points of capital weighted by likelihood.
    Severity
    ≥10 high · ≥4 medium · below 4 low
    Applied per risk, on the exposure figure.
    Capital at risk
    capital × score range
    Low side from the weighted total; high side adds the combined impact of risks at or above 50% probability.
    Stress test
    probability × (1 + s)
    s runs from −50% to +50%. The saved analysis is never overwritten.

    What the model sees

    Your description, plus capital, timeline, sector and market if you supply them. No live market data, no company filings, no prices.

    What it cannot do

    It cannot see risks you did not describe, and it treats risks as independent when they often correlate. Totals are approximate, not actuarial.

    How to challenge it

    Read the three largest exposures, ask whether each probability is plausible, change one assumption and rerun, then compare the two versions.

    Every analysis is a structured estimate, not a forecast and not advice. Validate the assumptions with someone qualified before committing capital.

    Ownership

    Equity

    Equity is a residual claim on a company: shareholders are paid after everyone else, which is why the upside is uncapped and the downside reaches zero. Valuation is a forecast dressed as arithmetic, so the assumptions matter more than the decimal places.

    What moves it

    • Cash flow growth and the reinvestment needed to fund it
    • The discount rate, which carries both the risk-free rate and a risk premium
    • The terminal value, which usually carries most of the answer
    • How much of the value is already in the price

    What to watch

    • Revenue growth outrunning cash conversion for several periods
    • Rising share count from dilution
    • A terminal growth rate above nominal GDP

    Related risks

    Model risk ↗ Market risk ↗ Strategic risk ↗

    Fixed income

    Debt

    Debt is a contractual claim: fixed payments, a maturity date, and priority over equity. The quoted yield is only realised if every payment is made and reinvested at that same rate, which is why yield and duration are read together.

    What moves it

    • The level and shape of the yield curve
    • Credit spread for the issuer's risk
    • Reinvestment rates on coupons
    • Call and prepayment features

    What to watch

    • Price well above par with a low coupon — the yield is doing more work than it looks
    • Long duration in a rising-rate environment
    • Covenants that weaken the claim without changing the yield

    Related risks

    Interest rate risk ↗ Credit risk ↗ Reinvestment risk ↗

    Debt and equity

    Hybrid

    A hybrid pays a coupon like debt but carries an option to convert into equity. The investor is buying a bond floor plus a call option, and the premium over conversion value is what that option costs.

    What moves it

    • Share price relative to the conversion ratio
    • Implied volatility of the underlying equity
    • Credit quality of the issuer, which sets the bond floor
    • Call and reset terms

    What to watch

    • Conversion premium that only makes sense with high volatility
    • Issuer credit deteriorating while the equity looks attractive
    • Forced conversion triggers

    Related risks

    Interest rate risk ↗ Market risk ↗ Credit risk ↗

    Contracts

    Derivatives

    A derivative is a contract whose value comes from something else. Options give a right without an obligation, which caps the loss at the premium and makes the break-even the only number that matters.

    What moves it

    • Distance between strike and spot
    • Implied volatility and time to expiry
    • Cost of carry and dividends
    • Position size relative to the capital behind it

    What to watch

    • Premium that requires an implausible move to break even
    • Short options with uncapped downside
    • Margin calls arriving before the thesis plays out

    Related risks

    Market risk ↗ Counterparty risk ↗ Liquidity risk ↗

    Collectives

    Pooled funds

    A pooled fund spreads ownership across many holdings and charges for the management. The fee looks small in one year and is decisive over twenty, because it is charged on the whole balance every year.

    What moves it

    • Expense ratio and any performance fee
    • Gross return of the underlying holdings
    • Time horizon, which turns a small drag into a large one
    • Tax and platform charges outside the fund

    What to watch

    • Performance fees with no high-water mark
    • A fund that quietly changed its mandate
    • Fee layers: platform, fund, and trading costs

    Related risks

    Model risk ↗ Market risk ↗ Concentration risk ↗

    Structured

    Asset-backed

    Asset-backed securities pass through the cash flows of a loan pool, split into tranches. Senior tranches are paid first and absorb losses last; the equity tranche takes the first loss, which is where the risk actually sits.

    What moves it

    • Default and recovery rates in the underlying pool
    • Prepayment speed, which changes the tranche's life
    • Attachment and detachment points of each tranche
    • Quality of the servicing and the collateral

    What to watch

    • Prepayment assumptions that shorten the expected life
    • Recoveries that rely on a rising collateral market
    • Correlation between borrowers in the pool

    Related risks

    Credit risk ↗ Concentration risk ↗ Liquidity risk ↗

    Liquidity

    Cash & money market

    Cash and money market instruments are the closest thing to liquidity, which is exactly why their return is measured against inflation. Bills are sold at a discount and repay at face, so the same holding has two different quoted yields.

    What moves it

    • Policy rates and the shape of the short curve
    • Inflation, which sets the real return
    • Credit quality of the issuer or deposit taker
    • Access and notice terms on the account

    What to watch

    • A discount yield quoted as if it were an annual return
    • Deposit guarantees that cap the protected amount
    • Real returns quietly negative for several years

    Related risks

    Inflation risk ↗ Liquidity risk ↗ Interest rate risk ↗

    Real assets

    Alternatives

    Alternatives are assets outside listed equity and debt: property, private markets, commodities and real assets. They are valued less often, trade slowly, and are usually judged on income yield plus capital growth.

    What moves it

    • Net operating income and the cap rate applied to it
    • Vacancy, tenant quality and lease length
    • Financing cost and refinancing risk
    • Liquidity: how long it takes to sell, and at what discount

    What to watch

    • A cap rate that reflects the market, not the purchase price
    • Capital expenditure deferred to protect headline income
    • Valuations based on the last transaction rather than the current market
